Rhode · Hailey Bieber

What if Rhode launched in India?

Rhode is built on glazed donut skin: dewy, minimalist, aspirational. But Indian beauty culture has its own obsessions. A straight copy-paste launch would flop.

The Insight
Indian Gen Z doesn't want Western glow. They want their version of it, rooted in their own ritual and reference points. Nykaa-native, not Sephora-imported.
The Idea
"Glazed, Not Whitened" — explicitly rejects the fairness-cream legacy. Lead with melanin-rich creators. Swap glass skin imagery for honey, amber, and gold.
Execution
Nykaa exclusive drop timed to wedding season. UGC challenge: #MyGlazedMoment. Seeded with mid-tier Indian creators first, not celebrities.

Spotify

Redesigning Spotify Wrapped for travelers

Wrapped tells you your top songs. It doesn't know you listened to Amaphouse in Lisbon and Bollywood on a train through Rajasthan.

The Insight
Music is memory. Travel is the sharpest memory trigger we have. People don't just want their top songs — they want their soundtrack mapped to where they were.
The Idea
"Wrapped: The Journey" — a location-layered Wrapped where your year in music maps to the cities you visited. Each destination gets its own shareable soundtrack card.
Execution
Opt-in location data. Partnership with Airbnb and Google Maps. TikTok format where creators share their city-by-city Wrapped stories.

How I think about marketing

Click to expand. These are the observations, opinions, and frameworks I carry into every brief.

01
Why nostalgia marketing works so well right now
↓
Gen Z is nostalgic for a time they didn't even live through. Y2K fashion, early internet aesthetics, Polaroid filters. Brands that tap this aren't selling products — they're selling emotional belonging to an era that feels safer than the present. Nostalgia is anxiety relief dressed as aesthetic. The brands winning with it (Jacquemus, Dr. Pepper throwback cans) understand that the feeling is the product.
02
TikTok trends brands should stop using immediately
↓
The "POV: you're our product" format. Forced trending audio with nothing to do with what the brand sells. Any brand that stitches a viral video and says "this is literally us." The problem isn't using TikTok formats — it's using them six weeks too late and without personality. TikTok rewards specificity and timing. Generic trend-chasing does the opposite of what brands think it does.
03
Brands with genius positioning right now
↓
Liquid Death turned water into a rebellion. Duolingo made a mascot threatening and built a cult following doing it. Rare Beauty launched on the back of a mental health conversation and never let go. What they share: they picked a lane that felt wrong to a traditional marketer and committed completely. The lesson isn't "be weird." It's: know exactly who you are, and never water it down for the people who don't get it.
04
The B2B storytelling gap nobody is fixing
↓
Most B2B marketing is written for the person who approves the budget, not the person who uses the product. The brands breaking through (Notion, Figma, early Slack) treat business users like humans with taste. They make product marketing feel like editorial. This is what I tried to do with the ESCsmart comic — the spec sheet exists, but the story is what makes someone actually care.
05
Why the Sabrina Carpenter era matters for brands
↓
Every brand wants Sabrina Carpenter right now and most are going about it wrong. She works because her persona is incredibly specific — the slightly chaotic, unapologetically feminine, self-aware girl. Brands that align with that specific energy win. Brands that just see "famous young woman" and slot her in lose. Celebrity partnerships only work when the celebrity's actual personality fits your brand's actual identity.
06
What comes after quiet luxury
↓
Quiet luxury peaked when people got tired of logomania. But now that Zara is selling "quiet luxury," the signal has broken down. What comes next is probably loud craft — things that are obviously handmade, obviously specific, obviously not mass-produced. The pendulum always swings back toward individuality when aesthetic movements go mass-market.
